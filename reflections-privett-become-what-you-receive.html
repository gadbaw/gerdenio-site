Become What You Receive

A homily by Stephen A. Privett, S.J.

Every dinner invitation risks refusal. A homily on the Eucharist as God’s own invitation — come, sit, eat with me — and on the communion we are asked to become.

Delivered June 7, 2026 — Solemnity of the Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ (Corpus Christi).

When we invite someone to dinner, we are not offering them needed calories. We are offering them ourselves.

To say come, sit, eat with me is to say I want to know you, and I want to be known by you. We clean the house, we set the table, we choose the food with care — not because the guest is hungry, but because we are. Hungry for connection. Hungry to share what we love with someone we hope will love us back. Every dinner invitation is, at its heart, a risk: the risk of being truly seen, and the possibility of being truly welcomed.

And there is always the possibility of refusal.

Imagine preparing such a meal — pouring yourself into it — and watching your guest sit down, look at what you have made, and say: I won’t eat any of it. They would be refusing more than the food. They would be refusing you.

This is what Jesus means when he speaks of those who have eyes that do not see and ears that do not hear. Not blindness or deafness of the body, but of the heart — the inability, or unwillingness, to receive what is being offered.

Because God is offering something here, at this table.

In a way that exceeds our power to explain, the simple elements of bread and wine become the vehicle of God’s own self-giving. This meal is not a religious formality. It is a divine invitation — God reaching across the distance between heaven and earth, risking rejection, saying: I love you. I want you to be my friend. Come, sit. Eat with me.

Too often, we seem like ungrateful guests — present in the room, but not sharing the meal. We have treated this table as a ritual to be observed rather than a gift to be received. But Jesus did not say, take and adore. He said, take and eat. Take and drink. Do this in memory of me. His body and blood were not given to be placed behind golden doors, but to be taken in, to become part of us — to nourish us for the actual work of living: for grief and failure, for doubt and struggle, for the long ordinary days when we need more strength than we possess.

Here is the mystery at the center of all of this: Holy Communion is not merely something we receive. It is something we become.

The word communion means a coming-together. And that is precisely what this table enacts. When we gather here, we are not a collection of individuals performing private devotions side by side. We are being made into one body — the Body of Christ. We believe what we eat, so that we become what we believe.

This is the one place on earth where none of the usual hierarchies apply. Wealth does not buy a better seat. Race earns no privilege. Gender confers no advantage. We come forward the same way — empty-handed, in need — and we receive the same bread. One table. One loaf. One body.

And here is where the invitation becomes a commission.

What we do here, God asks us to do out there. To live in communion with others. To treat all people as equally precious. To break ourselves open in the service of those who are suffering — just as the bread is broken here for us. The holiest thing in this building is not the tabernacle. It is the person sitting next to you. The Body of Christ is as close to you as the nearest human face.

The holiest thing in this building is not the tabernacle. It is the person sitting next to you.

When the minister says, The Body of Christ — and you say Amen — you are not saying yes only to the bread in your hand. You are saying yes to this community, gathered and broken and fed together. You are saying yes to the suffering body of Christ you see on your phone, in the news, in the stranger, in the person you find it hardest to love.

You are saying: I accept the invitation. I will sit at this table. And I will become what I receive.

May our eyes see it. May our ears hear it. May our hearts, at last, be opened — to God’s love offered freely here, and to God’s face hidden in the faces of one another.

Stephen A. Privett, S.J. is a Jesuit priest and president emeritus of the University of San Francisco, and a friend of the Center's founder for more than fifty years.
