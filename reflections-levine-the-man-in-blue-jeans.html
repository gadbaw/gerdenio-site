The Man in Blue Jeans

A poem by Max Levine, Psy.D., read by the author

Written after a triage intake at a transitional care clinic in San Antonio, where the author saw patients newly discharged from the hospital, most often after a suicide attempt. The details, he tells us, are all true.

This poem describes a suicide attempt. If you or someone you love is in crisis, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline).

Listen — read by the author

0:00 / 4:05

The man in blue jeans, and clasped hands and gauze bandaged cheek; filled the whole of the olive green chair in the corner of my office; and he was silent for a moment and so was I.

And he looked at me, and I looked at him, and the walls were white, and the hallways hummed; but the lamplight was soft, and quiet, and warm.

And outside the door, in the murmuring, and linoleum footfalls and phonecalls and keystrokes, there were many lives being shuffled with urgency, toward disposition.

And the man in blue jeans told me his name; and I told him mine; and we shook hands, and he smiled. And so did I.

And he called me doctor; And I told him no need. He just swept the floors he said, and apologized for the way he spoke. I shook my head— I was happy he came in, I said.

And he told me how he had reached the end; about the slights, and the debt; the anger and the grief; the cigarettes, and the loneliness; the love he had lost; the world he imagined, and the world he was in.

And he told me how he had climbed to a very high place; how he had looked down at the pavement; and the bushes and his own two hands. And he told me how he had felt unafraid; how he had let himself fall, how his ribs had groaned; how he had lain on his side; how his body surprised him.

And he told me he felt nothing; but then came the fear; and he was struck by the beauty he saw from down in that place: the streetlights reflecting in the red-pooled liquid of his life slowly leaving him; and the way it seeped into the cracks and filled the roots of pavement weeds, and the soil beneath him. And he told me about the ink black of the night sky, and the shining powerlines and the passing headlights— but most of all, he told me about the moon. He told me it was full, and bright; and beautiful.

He said it was so much all at once, that he could not seem to understand it, as if he’d never seen it before. He asked if I’d seen the moon like that. I told him I had.

And he said he had lain there a while, and that the world grew dimmer, and his body grew weaker, but the moon was still there. And he told me there couldn’t be something like that still in the world and yet he about to leave it. So he didn’t.

And he called out; and a stranger came, and he woke up somewhere else, in a bed, with blue sheets, and a bright overhead light; hanging above him, round and white. And he wept.

And he told me how he had asked the nurse for paper, and pencil; how he called his mother; how he had drawn her a bird; her favorite kind; the kind he knew she liked. And he told me how flimsy it felt; in the face of it all; like a child— a small drawing; asking forgiveness, for having almost taken her only son.

And there he was in my olive green chair.

And he smiled; and I smiled back.

And then the clock; and the tears, and the handshake; and the billing— the schedule; and the door; and the long drive home.

And then the walk from my car and the moon in the sky; the small bird on the powerline; the buzzing of my phone; a message from my mother: “Hi Max! Just calling to say hi.”

Max Levine, Psy.D. is a psychologist and poet, and a friend of the Center. He wrote “The Man in Blue Jeans” while working at a transitional care clinic at UT Health San Antonio; the recording is his own reading.
