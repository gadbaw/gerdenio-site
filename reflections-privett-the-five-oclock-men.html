The Five O’Clock Men

A homily by Stephen A. Privett, S.J.

Are you envious because I am generous? A homily on the parable of the vineyard — on the day laborers no one chooses first, and on a God whose favor was never wages for hours worked.

Delivered September 20, 2026 — Twenty-fifth Sunday in Ordinary Time.

Gospel · Matthew 20:1–16a

Jesus told his disciples this parable: “The kingdom of heaven is like a landowner who went out at dawn to hire laborers for his vineyard. After agreeing with them for the usual daily wage, he sent them into his vineyard. Going out about nine o’clock, the landowner saw others standing idle in the marketplace, and he said to them, ‘You too go into my vineyard, and I will give you what is just.’ So they went off. And he went out again around noon, and around three o’clock, and did likewise. Going out about five o’clock, the landowner found others standing around, and said to them, ‘Why do you stand here idle all day?’ They answered, ‘Because no one has hired us.’ He said to them, ‘You too go into my vineyard.’

When it was evening the owner of the vineyard said to his foreman, ‘Summon the laborers and give them their pay, beginning with the last and ending with the first.’ When those who had started about five o’clock came, each received the usual daily wage. So when the first came, they thought that they would receive more, but each of them also got the usual wage. And on receiving it they grumbled against the landowner, saying, ‘These last ones worked only one hour, and you have made them equal to us, who bore the day’s burden and the heat.’

He said to one of them in reply, ‘My friend, I am not cheating you. Did you not agree with me for the usual daily wage? Take what is yours and go. What if I wish to give this last one the same as you? Or am I not free to do as I wish with my own money? Are you envious because I am generous?’ Thus, the last will be first, and the first will be last.”

Lectionary for Mass, New American Bible, revised edition.

In Jesus’ time, day laborers were among the poorest of the poor. They lived hand to mouth — hired for a day in the fields, paid at sundown, with nothing put aside for tomorrow. No work meant no food.

Not long ago, we could still see that world outside our own doors. Men gathered before dawn in the Home Depot parking lot, hoping a truck would pull in and someone would wave them over. Today those parking lots are empty. Not because the need went away, but because the men are afraid to be seen. A day’s wage isn’t worth the risk of never coming home. The hand-to-mouth existence Jesus describes hasn’t ended — it’s just gone into hiding.

Today’s Gospel is a drama in three acts.

Act One: the hiring. At dawn, the landowner hires a group of workers and promises them the usual daily wage. He goes out again at nine, at noon, at three, each time hiring more and promising them “what is just.” At five o’clock, one hour before quitting time, he finds still more men standing around and sends them into the vineyard too — with no promise of pay at all.

Act Two: the payment. The workers line up, while the owner does something strange — he pays the last first. The five-o’clock men, who worked one hour, receive a full day’s wage. Word travels down the line. If they got a full day’s pay for an hour, what will the rest of us get? But when their turn comes, the ones who worked since dawn receive the exact same amount. They rightfully complain: this isn’t fair. We bore the burden of the day and the heat, and you’ve paid us the same as them.

Act Three: the conversation, where the parable explains itself. The owner isn’t unjust — he paid exactly what he promised. No one was cheated. Then he asks the question that unlocks everything: Are you envious because I am generous?

That’s the whole complaint. Not that they were shorted — but that grace was given to someone else, and it cost them nothing to receive it.

Picture that parking lot again, and look at who’s left standing at five o’clock. The older men. The ones with a bad hip or a bad cough. The ones who aren’t young or strong or fast enough to get chosen first. This is not a story from two thousand years ago — it’s this morning, in this city, among people some of us drive past every day.

The owner is fair — he pays what he promised. But he is also generous, and his generosity looks past what these men can offer him and sees instead what they need. The five-o’clock men are just as hungry, their children just as much in need of dinner, as the men hired at sunrise. They weren’t chosen last because they didn’t try — they were chosen last because no one wanted them first. And still the owner gives them a full day’s wage: enough for their family to eat tonight. Not because they earned it. Because they needed it.

They weren’t chosen last because they didn’t try — they were chosen last because no one wanted them first. And still the owner gives them a full day’s wage.

The parable begins, “The kingdom of heaven is like…” And whatever it’s like, it is not like our world. Isaiah tells us: my thoughts are not your thoughts, nor are your ways my ways… as high as the heavens are above the earth, so high are my ways above your ways.

This parable holds a mirror up to us. It asks: how much do our ways resemble God’s ways? Look at how this country treats the least — the poor, the undocumented, the unemployed, the homeless — and you will have a hard time finding God’s ways there. Look at who receives good medical care and who does not. Look at who goes hungry, unhoused, uneducated, uncared-for. This parable is a judgment on how we’ve arranged things. And it’s a call to arrange them differently: to be generous and compassionate, especially toward those with the greatest need and the fewest resources.

God’s favor was never wages for hours worked. God cares for us because God is good — not because we’ve earned it. And God asks us to hand on that same undeserved goodness, even to those we might be tempted to write off: fathers who cannot risk standing at Home Depot looking for work, families living in fear of being torn apart by raids that don’t care who deserves what.

God feeds us at this table for only one reason — not because we’re sinless, not because we’ve earned our place, but because we’re hungry and God is good. Let us ask for the grace to think more as God thinks, so that we act more as God acts, so that God’s will be done, and God’s kingdom come — among us, now.

Stephen A. Privett, S.J. is a Jesuit priest and president emeritus of the University of San Francisco, and a friend of the Center's founder for more than fifty years.
